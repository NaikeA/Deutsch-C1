GRAMMAR & PROFESSIONAL STYLE

Rules you can use immediately

Open a topic for the rule, contrast and examples. Focus on the choices that change meaning, tone or credibility.

01Commas: joining clauses and adding information

Use a comma before but, so, yet, for, and, nor when they join two complete clauses. Use commas around non-essential information, after an introductory phrase, and between items in a list.

  • The model converged, but the result was not physically credible.
  • After reviewing the evidence, we changed the boundary condition.
  • The senior engineer, who joined the review remotely, supported the recommendation.

Avoid joining two complete sentences with only a comma: “The test failed, we repeated it.”

02Semicolons: connecting closely related sentences

Use a semicolon between two complete sentences whose ideas are closely connected. It is stronger than a comma but less separate than a full stop. It can also separate complex list items.

  • The deadline remains unchanged; the scope, however, has increased.
  • Option A reduces cost; Option B improves reliability.

Do not use a semicolon before a dependent fragment: use a comma in “Although the risk is small, we should monitor it.”

03Colons, dashes and parentheses

Use a colon after a complete clause to introduce an explanation, list or conclusion. A dash creates emphasis; parentheses make information secondary.

  • We need one decision: approve the additional test.
  • The final result was clear—the original assumption was wrong.
  • The second run (completed on Friday) confirmed the trend.
04Past simple: completed events

Use the past simple for a finished action at a finished time. It is the normal tense for telling a chronological story.

  • We received the data on Monday and completed the analysis on Thursday.
  • I led the review and presented the recommendation.

Specific past time expressions—yesterday, last year, in 2024—normally require the past simple.

05Present perfect: past connected to now

Use have/has + past participle for experience, unfinished periods, recent results and past actions whose present consequence matters.

  • I have worked in Germany for thirteen years. (The period continues.)
  • We have identified the cause. (The finding matters now.)
  • I have led several international teams. (Experience, no finished time stated.)

Not: “I have completed it yesterday.” Say: “I completed it yesterday.”

06Past perfect: showing which past event came first

Use had + past participle when one past action happened before another past reference point.

  • By the time the review started, we had already corrected the mesh.
  • The result changed because the supplier had updated the input data.

Once the sequence is obvious, continue with the past simple; do not overload the story with past perfect forms.

07Imaginary present and future: second conditional

Use if + past simple, would + base verb for an unreal, unlikely or hypothetical present/future situation.

  • If we had more time, we would test another design.
  • If I were leading the project, I would clarify ownership first.

In formal English, were is preferred for all persons in hypothetical statements: “If I were …”

08Imaginary past: third and mixed conditionals

Use if + past perfect, would have + past participle for an unreal past. Use a mixed conditional when the imagined past changes the present.

  • If we had validated the assumption earlier, we would have avoided the delay.
  • If I had accepted that role, I would be living abroad now. (mixed)
09Real conditions: zero and first conditionals

Use the zero conditional for general truths and the first conditional for a realistic future possibility.

  • If the temperature rises, the material expands. (general)
  • If the customer approves today, we will start tomorrow. (real future)

After if, normally use the present tense—not “will”—for a future condition.

10Modal verbs: certainty, advice and obligation

Choose the modal according to the strength of your claim.

  • The change may affect the result. (possibility)
  • The change should improve stability. (reasonable expectation)
  • The change must be validated. (necessity)
  • The change cannot explain the full deviation. (impossibility)

For past deductions: “The input may have caused the difference.”

11Articles: a, an, the and no article

Use a/an for one non-specific countable item, the for something identifiable, and no article for plural or uncountable ideas in general.

  • We identified a risk. The risk affects the deadline.
  • Simulation supports engineering decisions. (general field)
  • The simulation we discussed is complete. (specific)
12Relative clauses: essential versus extra information

Do not use commas around an essential defining clause. Use commas around a non-defining clause that merely adds information.

  • The result that exceeded the limit requires investigation. (which result?)
  • The final result, which exceeded the limit, requires investigation. (extra fact)

Use who for people, which for things and that mainly in defining clauses.

13Reported speech and reporting verbs

Use reporting verbs to show the purpose and strength of another person’s statement.

  • The customer confirmed that the scope had not changed.
  • The reviewer suggested testing another condition.
  • The project lead warned that the deadline was at risk.

Useful verbs: acknowledge, argue, clarify, confirm, emphasise, recommend, report, suggest and warn.

14Hedging without sounding uncertain

Hedging makes a claim proportionate to the evidence. Remove apologetic fillers; keep precise limits.

  • Weak: “I’m not sure, but maybe this is wrong.”
  • Measured: “The current evidence suggests that this assumption is unreliable.”
  • Measured: “This is likely to affect the result, although further validation is required.”
15Parallel structure and readable lists

Items joined in a list or comparison should use the same grammatical form.

  • Parallel: Our goals are reducing cost, improving reliability and shortening delivery time.
  • Not parallel: reducing cost, better reliability and to shorten delivery time.

Parallel structure makes even familiar ideas sound controlled and memorable.

16Emphasis and inversion in formal English

Use fronting and inversion sparingly when the contrast deserves emphasis.

  • What matters most is the reliability of the conclusion.
  • Only after the second test did we identify the cause.
  • Not only did the change reduce cost, but it also improved performance.
17Active and passive voice

Use active voice when responsibility matters. Use passive voice when the process or result matters more than the actor.

  • Active: The simulation team validated the model.
  • Passive: The model was validated against test data.

Too much passive voice can hide ownership and make a message harder to follow.

18Sentence boundaries: fragments and run-ons

Every independent sentence needs a complete clause. Separate complete ideas with a full stop, semicolon or a conjunction—not only a comma.

  • Fragment: “Because the input arrived late.”
  • Complete: “The review was delayed because the input arrived late.”
  • Run-on: “The input arrived late, the review was delayed.”
  • Correct: “The input arrived late; therefore, the review was delayed.”
